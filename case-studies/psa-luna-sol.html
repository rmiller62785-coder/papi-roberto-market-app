01 — The problem

Thirty-three days from a fee change to an all-time record.

A submission-fee update on 14 May triggered a surge of roughly 20% — about 1.6 million extra cards in two weeks.1 By 2 June the queue neared 10 million and PSA paused all four Value tiers.1,3 By mid-June it stood at about 14 million cards, the largest queue in the company's history.4 PSA has not published the date of the peak; mid-June is the assumption this page works from, and the chart marks it with an uncertainty bar rather than a false coordinate.

Three things made this hard

A · Scale

Fourteen million units is not a problem you work harder at. Until the binding constraint is found and removed, every other effort is upstream of a wall.

B · Visibility

Volume held back by the paused tiers sat outside the counted queue. So a falling number proved nothing on its own — the metric could improve while the real position got worse.

C · Control

Reopening a tier too early would refill the queue within days. That decision needed a threshold and an owner, not a date in a press release.

02 — The blind spot

A falling number is not a recovery.

This is the finding the rest of the engagement is built on. The published backlog counts cards in the active queue. It does not count the submissions the tier pause was holding back — and those were going to arrive whether or not the queue was ready for them. A number that cannot see its own inflow cannot tell you whether you are winning.

A worked example of why this matters

One published fortnight does not close on grading alone.

Take the two weeks from the mid-June peak to 30 June. The queue fell 2.00M. June's reported output was 2.50M across the month7 — about 1.17M over fourteen days. Grading therefore accounts for 1.17M of a 2.00M fall, and the remaining 0.83M came off the count some other way.

Δqueue   =  12.00M − 14.00M   =  −2.00M
graded   =  2.50M × 14/30     =   1.17M
arrivals =  Δqueue + graded   =  −0.83M   ← cannot happen

Arrivals cannot be negative. So one of three things is true: volume left the count without being graded (cancellations, withdrawals, reclassification), the two readings are measured on different bases, or "mid-June" is not the date it appears to be. All three are ordinary. None of them is visible in the headline number.

That is the whole argument for the engagement. A measurement system that cannot surface an 0.83M discrepancy in its own public series is not a system you can run a recovery on. Finding holes like this one is what the work was for.

03 — On the ground

The day the line turned.

None of this was done from a deck. The measurement system was built against the real constraint, in the building where the cards actually move — capacity walked, queues counted, handoffs timed.

The photograph was taken at PSA on 30 June 2026, the same reading at which the published queue stopped climbing and turned down, from about 14 million toward 12 million.5 The coincidence of dates is a coincidence; a queue of that size turns on thousands of operating decisions, not on a visit. What the date marks is the point at which the measurement was in place and the recovery had something to be run against.

04 — The system

Three moves, and they only work in this order.

You cannot govern a number you do not trust, and you cannot trust a number until you know what constrains it. So: find the constraint, turn judgment into rules, then put the rules under an owner.

Move 01

Find the constraint that actually binds

Separate rated capacity from effective throughput, and locate where flow really caps out.

  • Capacity against realised throughput, stage by stage
  • Backlog segmented into categories you can act on
  • Held volume identified outside the counted queue
Move 02

Turn judgment into decision rules

Replace "how does it look this week?" with defined signals tied to specific decisions.

  • Leading and lagging indicators, kept separate
  • Thresholds with pre-agreed escalation triggers
  • Prioritisation driven by queue behaviour, not by pressure
Move 03

Make the recovery governable

Every signal gets an owner, a cadence and a defined response. Reopening is earned, not scheduled.

  • Owners, cadence and review mechanisms
  • Reopening gated on evidence rather than on a calendar
  • Recurrence controls for the next surge
What changed in the room
Before
  • "How does the number look this week?"
  • One count, read as a verdict
  • Reopening tied to a target date
  • Escalation when someone raises it
  • Recovery judged by direction of travel
After
  • Five signals, each answering a stated question
  • Level, velocity, aging, held volume and constraint capacity, read together
  • Reopening gated on a threshold, with a named owner
  • Escalation triggered by a breach, not by a person
  • Recovery judged against the path, at every reading
05 — The stress test

Then the queue went back up.

On 14 July the backlog touched 11.0M.6 Two weeks later it was 12.4M — up 1.4 million — as held-back volume returned.2 This is the fortnight the whole system was built for, and it is the reason the chart at the top of this page stopped where it did.

What a calendar promise would have cost

A date would have broken here, in public.

Any commitment of the form "Value tiers reopen when the backlog clears 11 million" — or worse, "reopen on 1 August" — was satisfied on 14 July and violated by 28 July. Reopening on that signal would have released held volume into a queue that was already refilling, on the reading immediately before it rose 1.4 million.

Threshold logic treats the rebound as what it is: a signal to review the root cause and keep reopening gated on evidence. That pattern was the latent-volume risk identified during the engagement.8 By 11 August the queue had eased to 11.85M.2

Recovery is not a straight line, and a page that showed one would be lying. The value of the system is knowing, at every reading, whether you are still on the path — and being able to show your work when someone asks.

06 — Where it stands

The queue fell 15%. The time to clear it fell 36%.

That gap is the result, and most of it came from the denominator. The queue fell to 0.85 of its peak; monthly output rose about 33% over the same span. Multiply the two and cover falls 36% — roughly two thirds of that from output, one third from the drawdown.

One limit on the 36%: it rests on a derived May output (2.50 ÷ 1.21) and a July floor (2.50 × 1.10), so it is a ratio of two published series rather than a measured quantity. Both figures move if PSA publishes the actuals.
